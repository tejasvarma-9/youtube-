HOW TO USE THIS FILE: use only the figures in section A and the quotes in section B. Section C must be confirmed by the fact-checker before use; if it cannot be, cut it. Do not add any card figure that is not in this file. Name the source and year every time a figure is given; the sources cover different years, so never add or compare figures from different years as if they were one year. THE ANGLE (our read, say it once as "one way to read this"):
Rewards are not a gift from the bank. Two groups pay for them. First, merchants: they pay a fee on every card sale, most of it goes to the bank that issued the card, and the CFPB says issuers typically use that money to cover rewards. Second, cardholders who carry a balance: they pay almost all the interest and fees but get a small share of the rewards. So the person collecting 2% back is often being paid, in part, by people who never see it.

A. FIGURES CHECKED BY ME AGAINST THE PRIMARY SOURCE
A1. Rewards and who gets them (CFPB, "Credit Card Rewards" Issue Spotlight, May 2024)
- "Consumers earned more than $40 billion in rewards on major issuers' general purpose credit cards in 2022."
- "the average value of rewards earned has also grown from 1.4 cents per dollar spent in 2019 to 1.6 cents in 2022."
- "cardholders who revolve debt from one cycle to the next pay 94 percent of total interest and fees" but "gain less than 30 percent of rewards benefits". ("Revolve" means carry a balance from one month to the next. Say that in plain words.)
A2. The size of interest and fees (CFPB, "The Consumer Credit Card Market", December 2025 edition, 2024 data)
- "In 2024, consumers were assessed $160 billion in interest charges, up from $105 billion in 2022."
- "consumers paid $31.3 billion in fees, up 23 percent from 2022."
- The share of accounts that carry a balance "returned to prepandemic levels of around 50 percent".
- "the average annual percentage rate (APR) reached 25.2 percent for general purpose cards".
- Purchase volume on consumer credit cards was $3.6 trillion in 2024.
A3. Where the merchant's fee goes (CFPB, "The Consumer Credit Card Market", October 2023 edition)
- Interchange (the fee the merchant's bank pays to the card-issuing bank on each sale) has stayed at about 1.8 percent of purchase volume. Say "about 1.8 percent", attributed to the CFPB, about 2022.
- Footnote 42: "issuers typically use interchange revenue to cover the cost of both interchange expense and rewards expense."
- Rewards earned by general purpose cardholders "exceeded $40 billion for mass market issuers in 2022."
A4. The networks don't pay your rewards (Visa 10-K, fiscal year ended 30 Sept 2025)
- "We do not issue cards, extend credit or set rates and fees for account holders of Visa products". The same sentence goes on to say Visa does not earn revenue from or bear credit risk for those activities.
- "Generally, IRFs are paid by acquirers to issuers." (IRF is Visa's name for interchange: the merchant's bank pays the card-issuing bank.)
- Visa net revenue: $40,000 million in fiscal 2025 ($35,926 million in fiscal 2024). Visa's money comes from processing and network fees, not from interest or rewards.
A5. One company where you can see both sides (American Express 10-K, 2025, $ millions; Amex issues cards and runs its own network, so it is a special case: say so)
- Discount revenue (the fee merchants pay Amex): 37,401. Card Member rewards: 18,409. Net interest income: 17,364. Net card fees: 9,993. Total revenues net of interest expense: 72,229.
- Our arithmetic (say "about"): Amex's rewards cost was about half of what merchants paid it in discount revenue.
A6. Federal Reserve working paper (Agarwal, Presbitero, Silva and Wix, "Who Pays For Your Rewards? Redistribution in the Credit Card Market", FEDS 2023-007, January 2023; based on 2019 bank data; a working paper, not a final peer-reviewed result: say "a Federal Reserve working paper")
- "regardless of income, sophisticated individuals profit from reward credit cards at the expense of naive consumers."
- "reward cards induce more spending, leaving naive consumers with higher unpaid balances."
- "Banks incentivize the use of reward cards by offering lower interest rates than on comparable cards without rewards."
- "We estimate an aggregate annual redistribution of $15 billion from less to more educated, poorer to richer" areas. Use the paper's own words; do not add a moral judgement. "Naive" and "sophisticated" are the authors' terms: explain them as people who carry balances and pay interest versus people who pay in full and collect rewards.
A7. The old cash-payer estimate (Boston Fed, Schuh, Shy and Stavins, "Who Gains and Who Loses from Credit Card Payments?", 2010, using 2007 data and assumed fee and reward rates). Use only as history, labelled "a 2010 Boston Fed estimate".
- "On average, each cash-using household pays $149 to card-using households" and "each card-using household receives $1,133 from cash users every year."
- In this paper "cash" means everything other than credit cards (cash, checks, debit cards). Say so if used.
- The lowest-income household ($20,000 or less a year) "pays $21" and the highest-income household ($150,000 or more) "receives $750 every year", after rewards.
- It assumed merchants charge everyone the same price; since then US merchants have been allowed to add a credit card surcharge in many states (see C). So say the estimate is old and was built on that assumption.

B. WHAT THE SOURCES DO NOT SAY (the script must be honest about this)
- The CFPB says interchange alone typically covers rewards. So "people who carry a balance pay for your rewards" is too simple. The supported version: merchants' fees fund most rewards, and people carrying a balance pay almost all interest and fees while getting under 30 percent of rewards (A1). Do not say "debtors pay for your cashback" as fact.
- There is no current measurement of how much prices rise because of card fees, or how much a cash or debit payer pays today. Only the 2010 model exists.
- The Fed paper finds the average reward cardholder comes out slightly ahead; it is people carrying balances who lose. Don't claim most cardholders lose.

C. CONTEXT THE FACT-CHECKER MUST CONFIRM BEFORE USE (from research agents, not re-checked by me)
- CFPB 2023 report: revolving cardholders "pay almost all interest charged, and three-fourths of total fees assessed, but earn less than 30 percent of the dollar value of earned rewards"; and interchange covers rewards for both groups while revolvers' costs "still support the operational costs" of others' "free" cards. My fetch of that page was cut off; confirm the exact page before use.
- Visa's merchant rules: US merchants may surcharge credit (not debit) cards, capped at 3% and at their cost of acceptance; some states limit it. Visa guide dated 2023. Use only as "merchants can add a fee for credit cards in many states".
- Total US merchant card fees: Nilson Report says US merchants paid $187.2 billion in processing fees in 2024 (secondary, paywalled). Use only if confirmed, labelled as industry data.
- Federal Reserve data: the debit card fee from large banks averaged about 0.47 percent of the transaction in 2023 versus about 1.8 percent credit interchange. Debit fees are capped by Federal Reserve rule. Use only as a one-line contrast, after confirming.
- Fed G.19 (Sept 2026 release): 22.15 percent average rate on accounts assessed interest in Q2 2026; revolving credit outstanding about $1.36 trillion in July 2026. Confirm on the current release before use.
- Do NOT use: lawsuits or settlements, pending legislation, the Fed's debit-fee proposal, any claim about India or other countries, any card brand's rewards rates.
