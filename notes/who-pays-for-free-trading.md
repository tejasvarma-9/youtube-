Research brief: "Who Really Pays for Free Stock Trading?" (written 2026-10-14, US retail brokers; compares Robinhood, Charles Schwab and Fidelity the same way)

HOW TO USE THIS FILE: use only the figures in section A and the quotes in section B. Section C must be confirmed by the fact-checker before use; if it cannot be, cut it. Treat all three brokers the same way: same tone, same standard of evidence, nobody praised or blamed. No politics: do not discuss pending rules, bans in other countries, lawsuits, or the January 2021 trading restrictions.

THE ANGLE (our read, say it once as "one way to read this"):
"Commission-free" does not mean nobody is paid. A broker that charges $0 a trade is paid in three other places. (1) The trading firm that executes your order pays the broker for sending it, a fraction of a cent per share or tens of cents per options contract. (2) The broker earns interest on the cash and margin loans in your account. (3) You pay in the price you get, which is set by the trading firm, and the research shows that price differs between brokers. Then give the other side fairly: the brokers and trading firms say orders usually get a better price than the public quote, and the best academic study found payment differences do not explain the price gaps.

OPENING IDEA (puzzle that withholds the answer, per voice.md): Robinhood says 28.4 million customers hold funded accounts. Stock trades cost them $0. Yet in one quarter its transaction-based revenue was $776 million. If the customer pays nothing, who paid? Ask within the first 90 words, then the one welcome line, then reveal the three places the money comes from at about 60 to 90 seconds. No roadmap.

A. FIGURES CHECKED BY ME AGAINST THE PRIMARY SOURCE (say "as of" each date)
A1. Robinhood (Q2 2026 results release, 29 July 2026; quarter ended 30 June 2026)
- Total net revenues $1.31 billion ($1,308 million), up 32% from $989 million a year earlier.
- Transaction-based revenues $776 million: options $342 million, equities $129 million, event contracts $156 million, cryptocurrencies $100 million. (These four add to $727 million; the release does not itemise the other $49 million.)
- Net interest revenues $389 million. Other revenues $143 million.
- 28.4 million Funded Customers; $369 billion Total Platform Assets.
- Our arithmetic (say "about"): transaction-based revenue was about 59% of net revenue; options alone about 26% of net revenue.
A2. Robinhood in the SEC's words (SEC press release 2020-321, 17 December 2020)
- Robinhood Financial agreed to pay a $65 million civil penalty, neither admitting nor denying the findings.
- The SEC found that from 2015 until late 2018 Robinhood made misleading statements and omissions about its largest revenue source, payments from trading firms for routing customer orders; and that its "commission free" marketing hid the costs of its high payment rates.
- The SEC found Robinhood's inferior trade prices cost customers $34.1 million in aggregate, even after counting the savings from not paying commissions.
- Say the dates plainly: this covers 2015 to 2019. It is not a statement about Robinhood today.
A3. How Robinhood described it (Robinhood newsroom post, "Demystifying payment for order flow", 4 March 2021, by Robinhood Securities' President and COO)
- "To compete with exchanges, market makers offer us rebates." and "Market makers typically give you better prices than exchanges." Attribute both to Robinhood.
- "we earned an average of $0.0023 per equity share traded in the fourth quarter of 2020." Say this is a 2020 figure.
A4. Charles Schwab (8-K earnings release, 21 July 2026; 10-Q for the quarter ended 30 June 2026; $ millions, Q2 2026)
- Total net revenues 7,072. Net interest revenue 3,357. Asset management and administration fees 1,825. Trading revenue 1,215. Other revenue 342. 39.8 million active brokerage accounts; client assets $13.08 trillion.
- Trading revenue split (10-Q): commissions 528; order flow revenue 624 (options 434, up 62%; equities 190, down 4%); principal transactions 63.
- Our arithmetic (say "about"): order flow was about 51% of Schwab's trading revenue and about 9% of its net revenue; net interest revenue was about 47% of net revenue.
- Schwab 10-K (FY2025) risk factors: payments "received from market makers and exchanges in connection with the execution of client equity and options trades" "account for significant revenue".
- Schwab's order-routing page: "Schwab receives rebates" as part of "a common industry practice known as Payment for Order Flow"; "Any eligible rebates from a particular market center are not a consideration in order routing decisions."; "most orders result in rebates". Attribute to Schwab.
A5. Fidelity (Fidelity Brokerage Services' SEC Rule 606 quarterly report, Q2 2026; a regulatory report, not a financial statement)
- The report says Fidelity "receives payment for order flow (PFOF) for orders that are executed by this wholesale market-maker." For marketable equity orders "the payment rate is $0.0008 per share or less"; for marketable single-leg option orders "between $0.30 and $0.40 per contract."
- "Neither NFS, nor FBS, consider payment as a condition for sending more order flow to a market-maker."
- The wholesale market-makers named for equities include Citadel Securities, Virtu Americas, G1 Execution Services, Jane Street, HRT Financial and Goldman Sachs.
- Why it matters: many viewers assume the largest brokers do not take these payments; this is Fidelity's own filing. State it neutrally, as the filing says.
A6. Robinhood's own Rule 606 report, Q2 2026
- Names Virtu Americas, Citadel Securities, G1 Execution Services, Jane Street and HRT (equities) and Citadel Securities, Dash/IMC, Wolverine, Jane Street and Global Execution Brokers (options).
- States that the payment amounts shown "represent 80% of the payments received by RHS" (Robinhood Securities) and that revenue is shared with Robinhood Financial. Use only to say the report shows payments are shared inside the group; do not quote per-share rates (the report gives them in "cents per hundred shares" by venue and order type, and they vary widely).
A7. Execution-price research (Schwarz, Barber, Huang, Jorion and Odean, "The 'Actual Retail Price' of Equity Trades", Journal of Finance, volume 80, issue 5, pages 2507-2541, October 2025; also a 2022 working-paper news release from Washington University's Olin Business School)
- The researchers placed 85,000 identical trades at the same time through different brokers (six accounts at five brokers) and measured the prices received.
- Their own account of the results: execution prices "vary significantly across brokers"; round-trip costs, excluding commissions, ranged from about 0.07% to 0.46% per account; they attribute the gaps to "off-exchange wholesalers systematically giving different execution prices for the same trades", and find that differences in payment for order flow do not explain the gaps.
- From the 2022 Olin news release (working-paper stage): the authors say it is "very hard to compare the actual retail price execution quality of different brokers"; one author said that while differences are large in total they "may be small on the individual level"; the authors self-funded the study and lost about $23,000 on the trades.
- Say plainly that Fidelity was one of the better-ranked brokers in the 2022 working-paper version. Do not rank the other brokers by name; the Olin page's list is inconsistent about how many brokers were tested (six accounts at five brokers).

B. WHAT THE SOURCES DO NOT SAY (the script must be honest about this)
- No source says what an average retail investor loses per trade today. The only dollar finding about harm is the SEC's $34.1 million for 2015 to 2019 at one broker. The research gives percentages from a test sample, not a bill for the average customer.
- The academic paper finds price differences between brokers but says payment differences do not explain them. So "the broker's payment makes your price worse" is NOT supported. The supported version: your price depends on who executes your order, brokers differ, and the payment is one of the ways the broker gets paid.
- Brokers and trading firms say orders typically get a better price than the public quote. We have the brokers' statements (A3, A4) and the market makers' own claim (C) but no independent measurement of how much, today.
- Schwab and Robinhood report different things: Schwab reports "order flow revenue" as a line; Robinhood reports "transaction-based revenues", which is wider than order flow, and its release does not isolate the payments. Fidelity is private and gives no revenue figure. Do not compare their percentages as if they were the same measure.
- Not all of any broker's revenue is trading. Most of Schwab's revenue (net interest and fees) is not trading at all. Say so once.
