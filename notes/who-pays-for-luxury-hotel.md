Research brief: "Who Really Pays for a Luxury Hotel?" (version 2, written 2026-10-06; covers hotels in general and luxury, not one company)

HOW TO USE THIS FILE: use only the figures in section A and the quotes in section B. Section C is context the fact-checker must confirm before any line from it is used; if it cannot, cut it. Do not add any hotel figure that is not in this file. Do not name individual hotels except the Waldorf Astoria example in A5. Do not suggest any company behaves badly: describe how the contracts are written, in the companies' own words. Name the company every time a figure is given; never present one company's numbers as "the industry".

THE ANGLE (our read, say it once as "one way to read this"):
The name on a hotel is usually a licence, not an owner. The investor, fund or REIT that owns a hotel pays for the building, takes on the debt and pays the running costs. The brand collects fees charged on revenue, so they are due before the owner knows if there is any profit. Luxury is the extreme case: it needs more staff per room, it often can't be built without selling apartments, and owners still hand the running of it to a brand for decades.

OPENING IDEA (puzzle that withholds the answer, per voice.md): In 2014 an insurance company paid $1.95 billion for the Waldorf Astoria in New York. It then agreed that Hilton, the company that sold it, would keep running the hotel for the next 100 years. Why pay for a building and let someone else run it? Ask the question inside the first 90 words, reveal the answer at about 60 to 90 seconds. No roadmap, no channel intro.

A. FIGURES CHECKED BY ME AGAINST THE PRIMARY DOCUMENT
A1. Hotels in general: the big franchisors (all at 31 Dec 2025, from each company's 10-K or earnings release)
- Marriott: 1,779,936 rooms in its system (10-K). It says: "Consistent with our focus on franchising, management, and licensing, we own or lease very few of our lodging properties (less than one percent of our system)." Owned/leased rooms: 14,406.
- Hilton: 1,351,351 rooms (10-K). Ownership (owned or leased) 15,287 rooms; managed 264,760; franchised/licensed 1,071,304 (this last line includes timeshare in Hilton's grouping).
- Our arithmetic for Marriott and Hilton together: about 3.1 million rooms; about 29,700 owned or leased; about 0.9% (say "under one percent of the rooms of these two companies").
- Choice Hotels: "We are primarily a hotel franchisor operating in 49 states, the District of Columbia, and 50 countries and territories." 7,575 open hotels, 656,825 rooms. "We do not anticipate owning hotels on a permanent basis and we expect to target dispositions to a franchisee encumbered with a long-term Choice franchise agreement in the future."
- Wyndham Hotels & Resorts: 8,389 properties, about 868,900 rooms. "We boast a remarkably asset-light business model dramatically limiting our capital needs." "We license our brands and associated trademarks to over 6,200 franchisees globally, which provides for a highly diversified owner base."
A2. What the brand charges, in the companies' own words (these are different contracts; do not average them)
- Marriott franchise royalty: "typically range from four to seven percent of room revenues, plus for certain brands, up to four percent of food and beverage revenues".
- Choice: "Royalty fees typically range from 5% to 6% of gross room revenues, and marketing and reservation fees typically range from 3% to 4% of gross room revenues." (our arithmetic: royalty plus marketing and reservation fees is about 8% to 10% of room revenue.)
- Wyndham: "our direct franchisees generally pay us a royalty fee of approximately 5% of gross room revenue and a marketing and reservation fee of 2% to 4% of gross room revenue."
- Management contracts (the brand runs the hotel), one large owner's description, Host Hotels & Resorts 10-K for 2025: base fee "in most instances is calculated as a percentage (generally 2-3%) of annual gross revenues"; incentive fee "typically is calculated as a percentage (generally 10-20%) of operating profit"; agreement terms "range from 10 to 50 years".
- Park Hotels & Resorts 10-K for 2025 (another owner of luxury and upper-upscale hotels): "The base management fee for our hotels range from approximately 1% to 4% of gross hotel revenues or receipts", plus incentive fees.
- Hilton 10-K: base fee is "generally based on a percentage of the hotel's monthly gross operating revenue", incentive fee "generally based on a percentage of the hotel's operating profits, normally over a one calendar year period, and, in some cases, may be subject to a stated return threshold to the hotel owner".
- Caution for the script: a managed hotel's owner also pays the operating costs (A3), so a low fee percentage is not the owner's whole bill. Do not say luxury fees are lower or higher than midscale: no filing we found says so.
A3. Who pays the running costs
- Marriott 10-K: "Under our management agreements, hotel owners are generally responsible for hotel operating costs, including centralized programs and services."
- Hilton 10-K: "In general, the owner pays all operating and other expenses and reimburses any of our out-of-pocket expenses."
- Marriott 10-K: "Current and potential hotel owners must periodically spend money to fund new hotel investments, as well as to refurbish and improve existing hotels."
- Host Hotels 10-K: "Expenditures for these major repairs and improvements affecting the hotel building typically are funded directly by owners but may also be funded from the FF&E reserve account." The FF&E reserve is "typically 4-5%" of each hotel's gross revenues.
- Hilton 10-K: "Hotel owners with financial difficulties have been and may continue to be unable or unwilling to pay us amounts that we are entitled to under our existing contracts on a timely basis or at all."
A4. What the brand's own revenue looks like (full year 2025, US$ millions)
- Marriott: franchise fees 3,325; base management fees 1,322; incentive management fees 791; gross fees 5,438; owned, leased and other 1,679; cost reimbursement revenue 19,204; total revenues 26,186; operating income 4,141. Our arithmetic: gross fees about 21% of revenue; cost reimbursement about 73%; owned/leased/other about 6%.
- Hilton: franchise and licensing fees 2,780; base and other management fees 376; incentive management fees 313; ownership 1,233; other revenues 252; cost reimbursement 7,085; total revenues 12,039; operating income 2,693. Our arithmetic: the three fee lines are 3,469, about 29% of revenue; cost reimbursement about 59%; ownership about 10%.
- We have NOT found the companies' exact definition of "cost reimbursement revenue". Say: "the filings say owners are generally responsible for these costs", not "owners paid all of this".
A5. The luxury owners (US hotel REITs, 10-Ks for 2025)
- Host Hotels & Resorts: "As of February 20, 2026, our consolidated lodging portfolio consists of 76 primarily luxury and upper-upscale hotels containing approximately 41,700 rooms." "All our hotels are managed by third parties pursuant to management or operating agreements." "We are prohibited from operating and managing hotels by applicable REIT rules." "Approximately 89% of our hotels, by room count, are managed by their own brand managers, and 11% are managed by independent managers." Its brands include Ritz-Carlton, Westin, Hyatt, Hilton, Fairmont and others. Do not name Four Seasons as a Host hotel.
- Park Hotels & Resorts: "over 96% of rooms in our Core portfolio are luxury and upper upscale." "In order for us to continue to qualify as a REIT, independent third parties must operate our hotels." As of February 2026, 34 properties with about 23,000 rooms.
- Waldorf Astoria New York, Hilton press release of 6 October 2014: Hilton agreed to sell it to Anbang Insurance Group for $1.95 billion, and "Anbang will grant Hilton Worldwide a management agreement to continue to operate the property for the next 100 years". Say "in 2014" and stop there: we have not checked anything that happened afterwards.
A6. Luxury costs more to run (data for 2018, CBRE Hotels, published 17 Dec 2019, by Robert Mandelbaum and Mark Woodworth; a sample of hotels, not every hotel, and old, so say "in 2018 data")
- Salaries, wages and benefits: luxury hotels "36.4 percent of total revenue", the overall sample 30.7 percent.
- Gross operating profit margin: luxury 33.3 percent, the overall sample 38.5 percent.
- Departmental expenses: luxury 44.0 percent of revenue, overall sample 36.9 percent. Rooms revenue: luxury 59.3 percent of total, overall sample 69.8 percent.
- Meaning (our read): luxury hotels sell more than rooms, mostly food and service, and the staff cost takes a bigger bite.
A7. Luxury often needs apartments to get built (SECONDARY, Bisnow, 30 April 2026; quotes are the executives' own words, attribute them by name)
- Marriott's Jake Pinsof: "All the deals that I'm working on, and Marriott's working on in the U.S., for luxury hotels, they all basically have a mixed-use component. We're not doing any ground-up hotel on its own right now."
- Viceroy's Mark Keiser: "It just is the only way to make those properties pencil."
- Savills data in the article: the number of luxury hotels with branded residential components tripled between 2015 and 2025.

B. WHAT THE FILINGS DO NOT SAY (the script must be honest about this)
- The filings report system-wide numbers; Marriott and Hilton do not separate luxury hotels from the rest. Say so once, plainly. Do not invent luxury-specific fee rates or luxury owner profit figures.
- None of the filings states what share of a typical owner's profit the fees take. Do not make up a "who gets the dollar" split for a room.
- We found no filing statement that luxury contracts differ in fee level.

C. CONTEXT THE FACT-CHECKER MUST CONFIRM BEFORE USE (read by research agents, not re-checked by me)
- Host Hotels 2025 results (company press release, Feb 2026): total revenues $6,114 million; management fees $262 million (our arithmetic would be about 4%, but the line may not match the 10-K's own line); comparable hotel EBITDA margin 28.9%.
- Host Hotels CEO and CFO on the Q4 2025 call (transcript on AOL, secondary): luxury transient revenue up more than 10%; wages and benefits roughly 50% of operating expenses. The CFO said brand-managed hotels do a good job with talent, which is a point in the brands' favour: include it if used, for balance.
- Marriott 10-K: owners can end some management agreements if Marriott misses performance metrics; Hilton says the same about some contracts. Marriott also makes loans and gives guarantees to owners. These balance the story: owners are not powerless, and brands carry some risk.
- Brands that own more of their own hotels: Mandarin Oriental says it has an equity interest in 12 of its 41 hotels (annual report 2024); Peninsula's parent appears to own most of its flagship hotels. Choice owns about 17 hotels. Use only to say "some operators do own hotels", and only after checking.
- Savills figure, the Bisnow quotes and the CBRE sample are secondary sources; confirm the original before putting numbers on screen.
- Do NOT use: any Accor, IHG or Hyatt figure; any claim about who owns Four Seasons, Aman or Ritz-Carlton hotels; the "51 owners wrote to Marriott" report; any court case; per-room renovation costs (the only figures found are from 2017); an industry-wide "share of hotels owned by REITs" (not found); total US hotel counts (sources disagree).

D. ILLUSTRATION (allowed, label it "a rough illustration, not a real hotel")
Take the range "2 to 3 percent of gross revenues" for a base management fee. On a hotel that takes in $100 million in a year, that is $2 million to $3 million to the manager, worked out before the owner pays staff, debt and repairs. Do not add a profit figure.
